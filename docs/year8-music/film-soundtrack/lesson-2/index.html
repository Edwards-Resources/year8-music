Music and Emotion

Lesson 2 of 25 · Sound and Story

We are learning how music shapes the emotions an audience feels.

Notes first.

Write the heading Non-diegetic music in your book. Copy the definition below, then the four ways music carries emotion. You will use these four words in every listening lesson this term.

Key word: Non-diegetic music

Music added for the audience that the characters in the story cannot hear. It is also called underscoring: background music written to support the action, mood or dialogue of a scene.

Liner notes

There is no sad instrument. There is only what a composer does with it.

A cello can open a funeral or a comedy. Ask most people why a scene feels tense and they will point at the instrument, but the instrument is close to the last thing that decides it. Emotion in a score comes from a small set of choices, and you already know all of them, because they are the same elements you used to build your track last term. What changes this term is the job they are doing. Last term they served a song. This term they serve a picture.

Four controls do most of the work. They are the four words you will use every time you describe a score this term.

  • Tempo sets whether you are pushed forward or held still.
  • Dynamics decide whether the music demands your attention or makes you lean in towards it.
  • Tone colour is the sound of the instruments and how they are played. A cello bowed low and slow sounds dark, the same cello plucked high sounds playful, and a synthesiser sounds like no instrument anyone is holding.
  • Texture is how many layers are sounding at once. One voice alone is exposed, a full orchestra is overwhelming, and music that starts thin and keeps adding layers feels like something is coming.

Two more sharpen them. Register is how high or low the music sits, which is why a piccolo and a double bass playing the same tune feel nothing alike. Harmony settles you or unsettles you: major tends to resolve, minor tends not to, and notes that clash refuse to.

The controls stack, and that is where the real work happens. High and soft is fragile. High and loud is alarm. Slow and minor reads as grief, while slow and major can be peace or dread depending entirely on what is on the screen. That last one matters most, because the picture carries half the meaning. The same thirty seconds of music under a wedding and under an empty road are not the same cue.

Your task - Screen off

Your teacher blanks the screen and plays about a minute of each piece. No titles, no pictures, just the music.

  • For each piece, write one or two sentences: what is happening in the film playing in your head?
  • Then name one of the four elements (tempo, dynamics, tone colour or texture) that put that picture in your head.
  • After each piece, compare with the people around you. How close were your films?

Why everyone imagines the same film.

Classes hearing the same minute of music very often imagine nearly the same scene, and students have been giving the same answers for years. That is not a coincidence. Composers use tempo, dynamics, tone colour and texture in ways an audience has learnt to read, so the music tells you the story before a single picture appears.

Need a hand? Word bank

Use these words: tense, playful, sweeping, gentle, ominous, hopeful, urgent, tender. Sentence starter: “The music feels ... because the ... makes it sound that way.”

Go further

Find another recording of one of today's themes - a different orchestra, a solo piano arrangement, a cover. Does the emotion survive the change, or does it need the original instrumentation?

Encore

Which piece put the clearest film in your head, and which element did it?
