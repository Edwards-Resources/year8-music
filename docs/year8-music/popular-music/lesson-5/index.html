Lesson 5: Popular Chord Progressions

Lesson 5 of 25, in Introduction and Drums.

Learning intention

We are learning how chords create harmony in popular songs.

Success criteria

Tick them off as you go.

Learn

Chord numerals are not chord names. They are seat numbers in a scale.

Look down the table below and the same progression turns up twice, once as C - G - Am - F and once as G - D - Em - C. Different letters, same progression. That is the whole reason musicians write chords as numerals instead of letters: the numeral describes the shape, and the shape is what songs actually share. Two bands playing in different keys are playing the same four chords in the sense that matters.

Number the notes of a scale from one to seven. In C major that is C, D, E, F, G, A, B. A chord numeral tells you which of those notes the chord is built on, so I is the chord on note one, V is the chord on note five, and vi is the chord on note six. Change key and every letter moves, but the seats stay where they are.

The case of the numeral carries one more piece of information, and it is easy to miss.

  • A capital numeral is a major chord: I, IV and V.
  • A lowercase numeral is a minor chord: ii, iii and vi.

That is why the pop progression is written I-V-vi-IV rather than I-V-VI-IV. The third chord is the minor one, and it is the reason a progression built from three bright chords still has some shadow in it.

ListenHear each progression

LearnSay it out loud.

How does it feel? Which elements make it feel like that? Answer as a class before you move on. The feel chart on the wall has words if you need them.

Your task

Play I-V-vi-IV on keyboard or guitar as a whole-class jam, then practise it on your own instrument. Four beats per chord, round and round.

Key words

Chord progression
A sequence of chords that provides the harmony of a song. The same progression can appear in completely different genres - what changes is the instruments, tempo and production around it.

Need a hand?Start with two chords

Play just C and G, four beats each, swapping back and forth. That is a real progression and plenty of songs use only two chords. On keyboard, C major is the three white notes C-E-G played together. G major is G-B-D. Find them, hold them, and count four.

Go further

Try ii-V-I, or substitute a chord in I-V-vi-IV for one not in the table. What happens to the mood? Then work out the progression of a song you like by ear.

Exit ticket

Why do you think so many pop songs use the same four chords?
