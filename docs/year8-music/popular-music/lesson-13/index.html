Hooks and Riffs

Lesson 13 of 25 · Bass and Hooks

We are learning the difference between a riff and a hook, and what makes each one stick.

Riffs and hooks in five songs

Riff on guitar - AC/DC, T.N.T.

Hook in the lyrics - The Easybeats, Friday On My Mind

Riff on strings - The Veronicas, Untouched

Riff on flute, and the song's hook - Men At Work, Down Under

Riff on a xylophone sound - Gotye, Somebody That I Used To Know

Liner notes

Every riff can become a hook. Not every hook is a riff.

A riff is a short instrumental pattern, usually two to four bars long, that repeats through a song. Three things make it a riff: it is short, it is played rather than sung, and it comes back. It is built from the notes of the chords underneath it, and it usually sits under or between the vocals rather than on top of them. The guitar figure that opens AC/DC's T.N.T. is a riff. It is under the singing for most of the song, and it is still the first thing you would play if someone asked you to play that song.

A hook is any part of a song written to catch the listener and stay in their head. It is named for the job it does, not for what plays it, so a hook can be sung, played, sampled or purely rhythmic. That is the real difference. A riff is described by what it is; a hook is described by what it achieves. A riff also repeats constantly, while a hook is often saved for the moment that matters most, usually the chorus, so that it lands instead of turning into wallpaper.

Hooks come in three kinds, and a song often runs more than one at once.

  • Melodic hook: a catchy tune, like the string line that drives The Veronicas' Untouched.
  • Rhythmic hook: a catchy pattern of long and short sounds, even on one note. The stomp-stomp-clap of Queen's We Will Rock You is a hook with no melody at all.
  • Lyrical hook: a catchy phrase, very often the song title. "Friday on my mind" lands at the end of the chorus every time.

The two overlap constantly, and that is not a fault in the words. When the riff is the most memorable thing in a song, the riff is also the hook. Down Under is the clearest case on this page: the flute figure is a riff by description, because it is short, played and repeated, and it is the hook by job, because it is the thing everyone remembers. What you cannot do is run it backwards. The sung title line in Gotye's chorus is a hook and it is not a riff, because it is sung and it does not repeat underneath the song the way a riff does.

What makes either one stick is the same short list: short enough to hold in your head, repeated often enough to learn, and simple enough to sing back after one hearing. Complicated ideas are rarely memorable. Notice that the five songs above carry their idea on five different things, guitar, voice, strings, flute and a xylophone sound. A hook is an idea, not an instrument.

In your book

  1. Rule up two columns, Riff and Hook. Copy the definition of each one from the liner notes above, in full sentences.
  2. Under each definition, write the three things that make it what it is. For a riff: short, played, repeated. For a hook: catchy, memorable, and named for its job rather than its instrument.
  3. List the three kinds of hook, with the example song for each.
  4. Write one sentence explaining the overlap: when is a riff also the hook?
  5. Choose two of the five songs above. For each, name whether you are describing a riff or a hook, and give one reason from your definitions.
  6. You need both definitions again in Lesson 15 and in the key words check in Lesson 25, so write them properly now.

Key word: Riff

A short instrumental pattern, usually two to four bars, that repeats through a song. Played rather than sung, most often on guitar, bass, keys or synth, and it usually sits under or between the vocals.

Key word: Hook

Any part of a song written to catch the listener and stay in their head. A hook is named for its job, not for what plays it, so it can be melodic, rhythmic or lyrical, and sung, played or sampled. When a riff is the most memorable thing in a song, the riff is also the hook.

Need a hand? Use the three-note model

Start from the teacher's three-note riff and change one thing - a note, the rhythm, or the instrument sound. That is still your riff, and it will work over your chords. Safe notes over a C-G-Am-F progression: C, E, G, A. Any of them will sound right.

Go further

Take your riff and flip it into another genre - play a rock guitar riff as an EDM synth line, or a synth hook on distorted guitar. Same notes, different world. What has to change besides the sound?

Encore

Sing your riff to the person next to you. Can they sing it back?
