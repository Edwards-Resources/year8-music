Same beat, different feel.

Your drum part in Lesson 4 played straight, even quavers. Jazz drummers swing them instead, splitting each pair long-short rather than evenly, so the beat rolls rather than ticks. Your bass in Lesson 11 held one root note per chord. A jazz bass line walks: it steps between the root notes of the chords, one note per beat, connecting one chord to the next instead of sitting still under it.

Liner notes

Jazz has a form too: head, solos, head.

A jazz performance usually plays the tune once at the start, called the head, so everyone knows what the song is. Then one or more musicians improvise over the same chords, taking turns, called the solos. The band closes by playing the head again, so the tune arrives and leaves in the same shape.

That is a section structure, the same idea as verse and chorus in the pop songs you have been recording, just built from three parts instead of several. Improvising in the solos section is not random: every note still has to fit the chords underneath, the same rule you followed programming your own bass and chords.

Your task - Hear the difference

Play back your own drum and bass tracks from Lessons 4 and 11. Where would swinging the drums change the feel? Where would a walking bass replace the held root note? Mark both spots.

Key word: Swing

A jazz rhythmic feel where quaver pairs are played long-short instead of even.

Key word: Walking bass

A bass line that steps between the notes of each chord, usually one note per beat, instead of holding one note per chord.

Need a hand? Tap it first

Before you try to hear swing, tap it. Tap four even beats, then tap the same four beats long-short, long-short. That physical difference is what your ear is listening for.

Go further

Try swinging your own drum groove in GarageBand and see whether it still locks with your unswung bass. Explain what has to change for the two to line up again.
